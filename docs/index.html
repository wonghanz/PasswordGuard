PasswordGuard

An open source Android app that judges a password, passphrase or email address on your phone. It does not ask how random your string looks. It asks how a guesser would reach it first — and reports the cheapest route it can find.

The mistake every meter makes

A strength bar multiplies length by character variety and calls the product entropy. For Summer2019 that is 59 bits, which reads as safe. The cheapest actual route to it is one word from a small list plus four digits — about 18 bits. The 41-bit gap is not rounding; it is the difference between a number and a measurement.

PasswordGuard prints both figures side by side, because the gap is the honest lesson. Once you have watched your own password get scored 41 bits too generously you do not go back to trusting a green bar.

Six routes, all on your phone

  • Lists, not luck — 216 common passwords and 221 English words ship inside the APK. Position in the list is the cost, so the reading uses it directly. A lookalike fold (0→o @→a 1→i 3→e) catches p@ssw0rd, and an affix check catches a listed password with a year stuck on the end, which is the most productive mutation there is.
  • Sequences and repeats — four or more keys along one keyboard row, any ascending or descending run, and the smallest unit that tiles the whole string. Every generator enumerates these, so treating them as free characters is what inflates the naive figure.
  • Calendar dates — eight digits, or separated triples, that parse on a real calendar. A birthday is the shape of a targeted attack, not of a random one.
  • Your own context — optionally type a surname, a birth year, a pet, a town. Bulk corpora cannot see these; an attacker who knows you can. Held in memory, wiped like everything else.
  • Email reading — a separate mode that refuses to give a band, because an address is not a secret. It reads the Gmail dot and plus rules that make an "alias" one mailbox, a birth year hiding in the local part, role names harvested by design, and throwaway domains.
  • Honest refusal — a non-ASCII symbol means the alphabet size is unknown, so the app says Inconclusive rather than print a number that would look like a measurement.

Every number, published

The reading keeps the smallest route, because that is the one an attacker takes. A −N figure means this route is N bits cheaper than treating the string as random; the figures do not stack.

Three refusals, and they are the design. No percentage anywhere. No time-to-crack duration, because turning bits into years means assuming a stranger's hardware and assuming they are attacking everyone rather than you. No breach lookup, because that means sending a hash of your secret to somebody's database to ask whether it is already there.

What it cannot see: whether your password has been exposed in a real breach; what a targeted attacker who knows you would guess beyond the context words you type; and anything at all about a password you did not enter here. Stated blind spots, not silent ones.

Bring your own key, structure only

No default provider, no free tier routed through anyone, no key in the repository. Settings takes a base URL, a model name and a key, stored in this app's private DataStore with allowBackup=false. What goes out is a structural summary — finding keys and bit deltas — and any finding whose on-screen text quotes part of your input is withheld from that path entirely. Point it at an ollama or llama.cpp endpoint on your own network and even the summary stays local.

Privacy, stated

  • Runtime permissions requested: none. Nothing is read from storage, contacts, accounts or the camera.
  • What you type is never written to DataStore, a file or a log. It is cleared when the app leaves the foreground.
  • FLAG_SECURE blocks screenshots and keeps the screen out of the recents thumbnails. There is deliberately no share target: accepting a secret from another app would put that app, and the share sheet, in charge of it.
  • Analytics, crash reporting, ads, attribution SDKs: none.
  • One residual limit, said plainly: a text field holds an immutable string that cannot be zeroed from application code, and a system autofill service may see keystrokes. This build cannot disable autofill at the Compose version it pins.

Build it

Requires JDK 17 and Android SDK platform 35. AGP 8.7.3, Kotlin 2.0.21, Compose BOM 2024.12.01. The debug APK is signed with the standard Android debug key. There is no backend, so there is nothing to self-host.

git clone https://github.com/wonghanz/PasswordGuard.git
cd PasswordGuard
export ANDROID_HOME=/path/to/Android/Sdk
./gradlew --no-daemon :app:assembleDebug
